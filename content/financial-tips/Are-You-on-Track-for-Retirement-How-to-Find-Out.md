**Key takeaways:**

* Independent research puts a “no-frills” retirement at roughly $36,700–$48,700 a year, and a “choices” (i.e. comfortable) retirement at roughly $41,100–$92,600 a year, depending on household size.
* NZ Super leaves a gap, from around $4,300 a year for a no-frills couple to roughly $48,200 a year for a couple wanting a comfortable lifestyle.
* A simple four-step framework can turn a vague concern about whether you’re on track for retirement into an actual number you can plan against.
* Small, early changes to your KiwiSaver contribution rate or fund type compound significantly over the decades before you retire.

## What does retirement cost in New Zealand?

Before you can work out whether you're on track for retirement, you need a genuine benchmark for what it costs. The most credible New Zealand-specific source is the [Massey University Retirement Expenditure Guidelines](https://financialadvice.nz/Common/Uploaded%20files/Research/Massey-University-NEW-ZEALAND-RETIREMENT-June-2025.pdf), updated in June 2025, which survey actual retiree households and split spending into two levels.

The first is “no-frills,” meaning the essentials plus a modest amount of leisure (groceries, running a car, basic home maintenance, a few hobbies and the occasional outing). It's a realistic, unglamorous baseline rather than a bare-bones survival budget.

The second is “choices,” sometimes called “comfortable.” Despite the name, this isn't a luxury lifestyle. It's the retirement most people picture when they imagine the word (a reasonably reliable car, regular meals out, an overseas trip every year or two, better health cover and room to help family or take up new interests without constantly checking the budget first).

For a metropolitan household, the guidelines put average weekly spending at:

| **Household** | **No-frills**                          | **Choices (comfortable)**                |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| One person    | $705.34 a week (around $36,678 a year) | $790.62 a week (around $41,112 a year)   |
| Two people    | $937.38 a week (around $48,744 a year) | $1,780.32 a week (around $92,577 a year) |

These numbers turn “enough” into a dollar figure instead of a feeling. If you've ever searched for how much you need for retirement in New Zealand, this is where the answer starts. Choose whichever lifestyle sounds closest to yours, and you have a real annual target to measure yourself against.

## How NZ Super fits in, and why it's not enough on its own

NZ Super is New Zealand's universal retirement payment, administered through Work and Income, and paid from age 65 regardless of how much you've personally saved or what KiwiSaver fund you're in. It isn't means-tested, so your other income and assets don't reduce what you receive. That's a useful design feature as it’s a guaranteed floor everyone gets to build on.

But a floor isn't a ceiling, and this is where the real number-checking needs to happen. As at 1 April 2026, the [official NZ Super rates ](https://www.workandincome.govt.nz/eligibility/seniors/superannuation/how-much-you-can-get.html)(tax code M) look like this:

| Category                       | Weekly  | Annual     |
| ------------------------------ | ------- | ---------- |
| Single, living alone           | $555.15 | $28,867.80 |
| Single, sharing accommodation  | $512.45 | $26,647.40 |
| Couple, both qualify, combined | $854.08 | $44,412.16 |
| Couple, both qualify, each     | $427.04 | $22,206.08 |

If you line those figures up against the retirement cost research, three gaps appear. A single person living alone wanting a no-frills retirement faces a shortfall of roughly $150 a week, about $7,800 a year. A couple wanting the same no-frills lifestyle is much closer, as their combined gap is around $83 a week, or about $4,300 a year.

A couple wanting a “choices” (comfortable, not extravagant) retirement faces a shortfall of roughly $926 a week against their combined NZ Super, close to $48,200 a year that has to be found from somewhere else (KiwiSaver, other savings, an investment property or continuing to work in some capacity).

The NZ Super amount was never designed to fund a comfortable lifestyle on its own. It's a universal base for everyone to top up. Knowing the actual size of the gap is what lets you plan around it.

## How to calculate whether you're on track

Whether you’re on track for retirement sounds like a yes-or-no question, but it's really a calculation with four fairly simple steps. A calculator, the numbers above and about 10 minutes will get you a working answer.

### Step 1: Estimate your annual retirement spending target

Use [the Massey guidelines](https://financialadvice.nz/Common/Uploaded%20files/Research/Massey-University-NEW-ZEALAND-RETIREMENT-June-2025.pdf) as a starting point. If you picture a no-frills retirement, use that figure for your household size. If you picture more freedom to travel and spend on things you enjoy, use the choices figure instead. Treat this as a useful starting point, not a fixed prediction.

### Step 2: Subtract your expected NZ Super

Take your spending target and subtract whichever NZ Super rate applies to your situation (single living alone, single sharing accommodation, or as part of a couple). What's left over is the annual gap that needs to be funded from savings.

### Step 3: Multiply that gap by your expected retirement length

If your annual gap is, say, $10,000, and you expect a 25-year retirement, your rough target is a lump sum of around $250,000. This is a simplified view (it doesn't account for investment returns continuing after 65 or inflation), but it gives you an order-of-magnitude figure to aim at rather than nothing at all.

### Step 4: Compare that target to your current KiwiSaver trajectory

Log into your provider's portal and check your current balance and contribution rate. A number of calculators, including Sorted's retirement calculator, can project your balance forward. If the projection lands near your target, you're broadly on track. If it falls well short, we’ll address that in the next section.

Any projection stretching 20 or 30 years out is inherently uncertain. This method isn't a forecast. It's a way to turn a vague worry into a concrete, revisable number, which is a far better place to plan from.

## The levers you can pull

If step four turned up a gap, the encouraging news is that a handful of KiwiSaver decisions, each fairly modest on its own, compound significantly over the years you have left before 65.

One lever is fund type. Being in a fund that doesn't match your timeline is a drag on a balance, one that rarely shows up as an obvious problem day-to-day. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much KiwiSaver do I need to retire in NZ?

There's no single number that fits everyone, but you can build your own using the four-step method above (target spending, subtract expected NZ Super, multiply by expected retirement length and compare to your current trajectory). It's a useful starting point rather than a hard target.

### Is NZ Super enough to live on in retirement?

For a no-frills lifestyle, NZ Super gets most people most of the way there, but generally still leaves a gap. For a more comfortable “choices” lifestyle, the gap is considerably larger, potentially several hundred dollars a week for a couple. It's a solid floor, not a full retirement income on its own.

### What's the average KiwiSaver balance for my age?

Average balances vary widely by provider and are published periodically by Inland Revenue and individual fund managers, but averages can mislead, since they're skewed by a relatively small number of very large balances. Your own target, based on your own spending and NZ Super entitlement, is a far more useful comparison.

### What can I do if I'm behind on retirement savings?

Start by getting a clear number, rather than staying in vague worry mode. From there, the main levers are reviewing your fund type, considering a higher contribution rate if you can afford it and checking you're not paying unnecessarily high fees for your fund's performance.

### Can I retire before 65 and still get NZ Super later?

Yes. You can stop working and draw on other savings, including KiwiSaver, before 65, and NZ Super entitlement simply begins once you reach qualifying age and meet the residency requirements, regardless of when you stopped working.

### Does my KiwiSaver fund type matter as I get closer to retirement?

Yes, it's one of the more commonly overlooked levers. Many people stay in the same fund type for decades without reviewing it, even though their timeline and risk tolerance have changed substantially. It's worth revisiting every few years, and especially in the decade before you plan to retire.

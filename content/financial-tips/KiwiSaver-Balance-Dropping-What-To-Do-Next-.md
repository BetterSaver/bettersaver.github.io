Is Your KiwiSaver Balance Dropping? What to Do Next

You open your KiwiSaver balance, expecting it to have gone up a little bit as usual. Instead, it's lower than last month. 

If you're seeing your KiwiSaver balance dropping, don’t panic, as there's usually a simple reason behind it. 

Balances move up and down because of how the money is invested, and a dip on the screen indicates a paper movement, not your money actually disappearing. What’s more important than this month's number is what you do next.

In this guide, we cover how market downturn affects KiwiSaver, why a knee-jerk reaction can turn a dip into a loss and what you can do if your balance is dropping.

Key takeaways:

A dropping balance usually reflects short-term market movement, not money that's disappeared for good.

Switching funds or cashing out during a downturn can lock in losses that would otherwise have recovered.

Continuing to contribute during a dip means your money (and the government's) buys in at a lower price.

Whether to switch funds should come down to fund fit (fees, performance and risk level), not fear.

Why your KiwiSaver balance can drop

KiwiSaver isn't a savings account that sits still and earns a fixed rate of interest. Most funds are invested in a mix of growth assets (like shares and commercial property), and the value of those assets moves with financial markets every day. 

The point is that growth assets let KiwiSaver grow faster than a bank account over the long run, but their value can also dip in the short term.

In a Growth or Balanced fund, more of your money sits in shares and property, which tend to swing more than cash and bonds.

&#x20;In a Conservative fund, your balance usually moves less, because more sits in lower-risk assets. It's rarely completely flat either, though. Check out our guide to 5 Types of KiwiSaver Funds and What They Mean for a breakdown of what each type holds and how that shapes the ride.

It helps to remember that KiwiSaver is large and well-established, not fragile. According to the FMA's KiwiSaver Annual Report 2025 media release, total KiwiSaver funds under management grew by 10% to $123 billion in the year to March 2025, and the regulator described the scheme as "resilient amidst economic volatility and uncertainty." 

Markets wobble for weeks or months at a stretch, but the system behind any market downturn KiwiSaver members go through has kept growing over time.

So when you see your KiwiSaver going down over a particular week or month, you're mostly seeing the normal texture of investing. It's not a sign that something's gone wrong with your account, your provider, or KiwiSaver itself.

Don't panic: why selling at the bottom locks in losses

A drop in your KiwiSaver balance isn't a loss until you actually sell or switch out of your investments. Until then, it's a paper movement. 

The price of the units you hold has gone down, but you still have the same number of units. If markets recover, as they historically have, your balance recovers with them, without you needing to do anything.

The danger is what happens when people react partway through a downturn. The government's independent money guidance service, Sorted, describes this by comparing a downturn to a wave. 

If you switch to a more conservative fund (or move out of KiwiSaver investments altogether) while you're partway down the wave, you can turn a temporary dip into an actual, locked-in loss. Then if you switch back once things look calmer, you often have to buy back in higher than you sold at, missing the recovery entirely.

A drop on screen isn't money gone for good, not unless you act in a way that makes it permanent.

Research reported by 1News, citing a Massey University study, found that roughly a third of KiwiSaver members who switched funds during a downturn ended up worse off than those who stayed invested. For most people, doing nothing was the better move.

Part of why people switch at the wrong moment comes down to psychology. As University of Auckland senior finance lecturer Gertjan Verdickt explains, people experience loss aversion, feeling a loss more strongly than an equivalent gain. 

If your KiwiSaver balance drops by a few hundred dollars, you’re likely to be more worried than you were satisfied when you experienced a rise of a similar amount. It's normal, but not a reliable guide to what to do with your account.

Should you stay in your fund or switch?

It’s best not to switch funds because you’re concerned about a market downturn. There's a real difference between a good reason to switch and a poor one.

Reasonable, evidence-based reasons to switch include consistent underperformance over several years (not one bad quarter), fees noticeably higher than comparable options, poor service or realising your fund's risk level no longer matches your timeline. 

Sorted's guide on how to pick the right KiwiSaver fund for you frames this well. The right fund matches your investment mix to your goals and timeline, not predictions about where markets are headed next.

A poor reason to switch is simply that your balance has dropped this month, or a headline about a market downturn has made you nervous about KiwiSaver going down more broadly. Reacting to short-term noise by moving a long-term investment is the pattern that locks in losses.

Continuing to contribute is crucial 

It's tempting to pause contributions when your KiwiSaver balance is dropping, but that's often the opposite of what's in your interests. The reason comes down to dollar-cost averaging.

Your KiwiSaver contributions buy units at whatever the current price is. When prices are down, the same dollar amount buys more units than when prices are high. 

Those extra units are still there when prices recover, so even though a downturn might feel uncomfortable, it can actually work in your favour if you’re still contributing regularly.

The government contribution is also a concrete reason why it’s better not to pause. Inland Revenue tops up your KiwiSaver by 25 cents for every dollar you contribute, up to $260.72 a year, on the first $1,042.86 contributed between 1 July and 30 June. If you stop contributing during a downturn, you risk missing some or all of that yearly top-up.

It's also a good idea to zoom out on how markets behave over time. According to Pie Funds CEO Ana-Marie Lockyer, markets move in cycles of growth, decline, then recovery and further growth. A downturn isn't a permanent state, but one phase of a pattern that has repeated many times during KiwiSaver's history. 

Over the 20, 30 or 40 years when most people are invested, a market downturn in any single year tends to be a blip, not a defining event.

When a downturn can be an opportunity

When unit prices fall, every dollar you and your employer put in, plus the government contribution if you qualify, buys more units than it would have a few months earlier so you're effectively buying in at a discount.

Continuing your normal contributions through a downturn puts more units in your account at a lower average cost.

That's one of the real advantages of a regular-contribution scheme like KiwiSaver. Contributions go in automatically, in good markets and bad, spreading your buy-in price across the ups and downs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal for my KiwiSaver balance to go down?

Yes. Most funds hold growth assets like shares and property, so their value moves with the market. Temporary dips are normal, not a sign anything is wrong.

Should I switch KiwiSaver funds when the market drops?

Not based on the drop alone. A switch should reflect fund fit for your goals and risk comfort. Switching purely in reaction to a downturn often means selling low and locking in a loss.

Will I lose my KiwiSaver savings if the market crashes?

Only if you sell or switch while prices are down. Staying invested means your balance can recover as markets do, as they have throughout KiwiSaver's history.

Should I stop contributing to KiwiSaver during a downturn?

Generally not. Pausing means missing out on buying units at a lower price, and risking some of the government contribution of up to $260.72 a year.

How long does it take for KiwiSaver to recover after a drop?

There's no fixed timeline, but markets have historically recovered over months to a few years. That's why KiwiSaver works best as a long-term investment, not one to check daily.

What's the difference between my balance dropping and actually losing money?

A dropping balance is a paper movement in units you still hold. You lock in a real loss only once you sell or switch below what you bought in at.

Keep calm, keep contributing

Seeing your KiwiSaver balance dropping is unsettling, but it's rarely a sign anything's gone wrong. It's usually just markets doing what markets do. What tends to cause problems is reacting to it with panic (switching funds near the bottom, or pausing contributions and missing the government top-up). 

Our advice is to keep contributing, and make sure you're in a fund that actually suits what you're trying to do, how long you've got and how much risk you're comfortable carrying.
